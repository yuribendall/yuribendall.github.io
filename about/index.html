About Me

A materials engineering student who thinks across the whole system.

No corporate buzzwords or generic resume templates—just a straightforward look at how I think about engineering, what I’ve built so far, and the kinds of problems I want to work on next.

01 · Identity

Who I Am

Why I combine mechatronics with a deeper foundation in materials science.

Most engineering programs try to put you in a single lane early on: either you write software, or you route PCBs, or you design mechanical brackets in CAD, or you study phase diagrams and crystal lattices in a lab. I never really bought into that divide.

What initially pulled me in was mechatronics—the intersection where mechanical structures and electrical systems talk to each other. Studying materials engineering on top of that feels like unlocking the underlying rules for both. Every actuator, sensor, airframe, and circuit board is ultimately governed by what it is made of: how its microstructure handles cyclic fatigue, how heat moves through a substrate, or how chemical etching shapes a conductor.

I’m happiest when I can move fluidly across the whole stack—modeling a system’s physics on my laptop in the morning, fabricating or etching the hardware in the afternoon, and inspecting how it actually performed under a microscope at night.

02 · Track Record

What I’ve Done

From closed-loop biological systems and hobby planes to custom PCBs, CFD simulations, and running a business.

I learn best by picking projects that push me into unfamiliar territory. Back in high school, that meant building a self-sustaining closed-loop terrarium for a research project to see how water, gas exchange, and porous substrate layers find equilibrium without external intervention.

In university, it meant joining an aero design team to build micro-class competition aircraft where every gram of balsa, carbon spar, and polymer film has to earn its place in the air—while spending my coursework analyzing specific rocks and their fracture surface features to understand how real, heterogeneous materials fail.

Along the way, I’ve etched custom PCBs from bare copper laminate for personal hardware projects, built robotics prototypes, run extensive aerodynamic simulations for vertical axis wind turbine (VAWT) blade performance, explored machine learning for physical modeling, and even started a company specifically to learn how supply chains, vendor lead times, and business logistics work in the real world.

03 · Direction

What I Want to Do & Why

Solving hard physical problems where materials, mechanics, electronics, and manufacturing meet.

A lot of modern tech treats hardware as an afterthought—just a commodity box to run code in. The problems I care about most—clean energy systems, aerospace structures, robotics, and resilient manufacturing—are the exact opposite. You can’t fake the physics, and you can’t ignore the supply chain.

What I want to do: Work on multidisciplinary R&D and hardware engineering teams where I can bridge materials characterization, mechatronic prototyping, and numerical simulation.

Why: Because the most interesting bottlenecks in hardware don’t happen neatly inside one department. They happen at the boundaries—where aerodynamic loads meet composite fatigue, where circuit layout meets chemical processing, and where a prototype in the lab has to survive real-world sourcing and production logistics.
